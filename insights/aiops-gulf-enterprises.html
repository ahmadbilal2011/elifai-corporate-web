AIOps for Gulf enterprises: where to start

AI for operations · 2 min read · Elifai engineering team · 8 November 2026

Every operations team in the Gulf is being asked what it is doing with AI. The honest answer for most is "not much yet", and that is a reasonable place to start. AIOps works best when it begins small, with a clear before-and-after measure, and with people in charge of every change to production.

Two use cases that pay back first

1. Alert correlation

A single failing database can trigger hundreds of alerts across the services that depend on it. Correlation groups them into one incident with a likely cause, so the on-call engineer starts from the problem rather than from the noise. This is usually the fastest win because the data already exists in your monitoring tools.

2. Incident summaries and ticket triage

Large language models are good at reading timelines, logs and chat threads and turning them into a clear summary. Used for incident reviews and for routing tickets to the right team, they save hours each week without making any change to production systems.

A safe six-week pilot

  1. Weeks 1 and 2, baseline. Measure what happens today: alerts per incident, time to acknowledge, time to restore, and hours spent writing incident reviews.
  2. Weeks 3 and 4, shadow mode. Run the AI alongside your team on real incidents. It suggests, people decide, and every suggestion is scored.
  3. Weeks 5 and 6, decide. Compare against the baseline. Keep what clearly helped, drop what did not, and agree which actions may ever be automated.

What to measure

  • Alerts per incident, before and after correlation.
  • Mean time to acknowledge and mean time to restore.
  • Share of AI suggestions engineers accepted.
  • Cost per incident handled, including model usage.

Guardrails for regulated environments

For Saudi and Gulf organisations subject to PDPL, NCA controls or sector rules, keep three guardrails from day one: run models where your data is allowed to be, which may mean a private model in-Kingdom; log every prompt and response used in an incident; and require a named engineer to approve any action that changes production.
