Choosing a cloud region in Saudi Arabia in 2026

Cloud strategy · 3 min read · Elifai engineering team · 8 November 2026

Two years ago a Saudi organisation that needed in-Kingdom hosting had a short list. By the end of 2026 almost every major provider will run a region inside the country. More choice is good news, but it also makes the decision harder. This guide sets out what is available, what the regulations actually ask for, and how we help clients choose.

Availability changes quickly, and not every service launches in every region on day one. Before committing, check the provider's own region page for the specific services you need, such as managed Kubernetes, GPU instances or a particular database engine.

What the rules actually require

Three sets of requirements usually shape the decision:

  • The Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL), enforceable since September 2024 and overseen by SDAIA, governs how personal data is processed and when it may be transferred outside the Kingdom.
  • The National Cybersecurity Authority's controls, in particular the Essential Cybersecurity Controls (ECC-2:2024) and the Cloud Cybersecurity Controls (CCC-2:2024), set expectations for how systems are secured, including those run by cloud providers and managed-service partners.
  • Sector rules from regulators such as SAMA for financial services, which can be stricter than the national baseline.

In practice, most of our Saudi clients choose to keep production data and its backups in-Kingdom by default, and document any exception. That removes a whole class of questions from every audit.

Four questions that decide the region

  1. Where must the data live? If the answer is "in the Kingdom, including backups and logs", rule out any design that ships telemetry or snapshots to another region by default.
  2. Which services do you actually need? A region that lacks your database engine or GPU type forces workarounds that cost more than a slightly higher unit price elsewhere.
  3. What do you already run? An existing Oracle estate often lands best on OCI; a team fluent in Kubernetes can move between GKE, EKS and OKE with little friction.
  4. How will you recover? Check whether the provider offers more than one Saudi region or zone, and plan disaster recovery inside the Kingdom if your rules require it.

Keeping multi-cloud manageable

Many Gulf organisations end up with two clouds and a private VMware or OpenShift estate. That is fine if the operating model is shared. We standardise on infrastructure as code with Terraform, GitOps delivery with Argo CD, one observability stack built on OpenTelemetry, Prometheus and Grafana, and one set of runbooks. The result is that an engineer on call does not need to remember which console a workload lives in.
